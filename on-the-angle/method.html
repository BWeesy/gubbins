How this works

What the numbers on On The Angle are, where they come from, and what they are not.

The two figures

The page shows one of two things, depending on whether Include reach is on.

FigureQuestion it answers
Goal face covered Standing exactly where they are, how much of the goal mouth is behind the keeper? Purely geometric: a shadow cast from the ball.
Weighted goal coverage The same, plus the parts of the goal they could still get to before the ball arrives — each counting for less the further out of reach it is, and less again if the part that would get there is a poor one to save with.

The keeper

The keeper is built from body proportions and equipment sizes rather than drawn, so the shape scales with the height slider. Kit dimensions are the maxima the rules allow, which is what a senior keeper wears.

Two postures, chosen by you rather than inferred: set, hands in front at chest height, and spread, gloves down on the floor either side of the pads. From the shooter’s view the forearms point almost straight at you and foreshorten to nothing, which is why what blocks is pads, body, helmet and two glove faces.

Not all of the keeper is equal

Getting a pad or the body behind the ball is a different proposition from reaching it with the stick, so each part carries a weight. The stick is drawn and counted in the weighted figure, but it never contributes to the plain goal face covered number — treating it as solid blocking area would flatter the position badly.

Scoring a point on the goal

For every point on the goal mouth, a line is drawn from the ball to that point and followed until it crosses the plane the keeper is standing in. Both the distance the keeper would have to cover and the time they have to do it are measured there, at the keeper, not at the goal line.

That matters more than it sounds. From close range a 10 cm movement by the keeper covers far more than 10 cm of goal line, and the ball reaches the keeper well before it would reach the goal. Measuring at the line would overstate the distance and hand them time they never get — and both errors are worst on exactly the shots where the answer matters most.

time available = distance from ball to the keeper's plane / ball speed gap = distance from that crossing point to the keeper, in their plane reach = (time available - reaction) x reach speed credit = 1 if the gap is zero, else the chance reach turns out to be enough

The reach up factor sets how far a keeper gets upwards against how far they get sideways. Below 1 makes high balls dearer than wide ones, above 1 the other way round; where a given keeper sits is their own, so it is a knob rather than a constant. The softness at the edge comes from the prediction bonus: read the shot early and you are moving sooner, get wrong-footed and you lose the same again, so the boundary fades instead of stopping dead. Set that to nothing and the whole thing collapses back to the plain covered-or-open picture.

What this is not

It is not a prediction of saves

The weighted figure averages over the whole goal mouth as though shots land at random. They do not: shooters aim at the corners and the gaps. At a position reading around 46% against a random shot, the four corners come out nearer 2%. Read it as a measure of the position, never as the chance of stopping the next shot.

Other things it does not do, all deliberate:

  • The ball flies in a straight line at a constant speed. Real drag flicks lift and swerve.
  • The keeper is flat. Gloves held in front are nearer the ball than the pads, so they really do cast a slightly larger shadow than they are credited with — a few per cent at close range.
  • Reach grows at a single steady rate. Nobody accelerates like that; this is a coverage figure, not a stride-by-stride simulation.
  • The stick weight of 0.35 is a judgement, not a measurement, and it rolls up two separate things: getting the stick there at all, and the deflection then going somewhere safe.
  • Nothing here has been validated against real saves. It is geometry with plausible constants on top.

Who wrote this

The tool was conceived by a goalkeeper, and every decision in it — what the figures should measure, what the keeper looks like, which parameters earn their place, what the numbers may and may not claim — came from the pitch. The postures, the kit dimensions and the spread height were specified and in one case measured off an actual keeper.

None of the code was written by hand. All of it was written by AI under that direction, reviewed and corrected against what a keeper knows to be true. Worth knowing if you are weighing how much to trust it: the hockey is answerable to someone, the implementation is answerable to whether it was checked.
